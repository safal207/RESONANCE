# Editorial Principles

These principles define how RESONANCE researches, writes, verifies, and corrects its work.

## 1. Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order when appropriate:

1. Primary documents, datasets, code, filings, papers, transcripts, official records.
2. Direct statements from identified participants or domain experts.
3. High-quality independent reporting.
4. Secondary analysis and commentary.
5. Anonymous or unverified claims only when clearly labeled and editorially justified.

Secondary coverage must not be presented as primary evidence.

## 2. Claim classification

Material claims should be treated as one of:

- **Verified fact** — directly supported by reliable evidence.
- **Supported inference** — not directly observed, but reasonably derived from evidence.
- **Disputed claim** — credible sources materially disagree.
- **Preliminary finding** — evidence exists but is incomplete or not yet independently confirmed.
- **Prediction** — a forward-looking judgment.
- **Opinion** — an explicitly subjective position.
- **Unknown** — available evidence is insufficient.

## 3. Verification chain

For consequential stories, use the RESONANCE verification chain:

**Signal → Claim → Source → Evidence → Cause → Timeline → Uncertainty → Verification → Implication → Action**

At each stage ask what would falsify the current interpretation.

## 4. Causality standard

Do not imply causation from sequence or correlation alone.

When making a causal argument, identify:

- the proposed mechanism;
- relevant preceding conditions;
- alternative explanations;
- evidence that supports or weakens the mechanism;
- the confidence level of the conclusion.

## 5. Time matters

A fact can be correct and still be misleading if its time context is wrong.

Record important dates for:

- when an event occurred;
- when evidence became available;
- when a source was updated;
- when our article was last verified.

## 6. Uncertainty must be visible

Do not convert uncertainty into certainty for rhetorical force.

Use language that reflects the evidence: confirmed, indicates, suggests, appears, remains unclear, disputed, preliminary, or unknown.

## 7. AI use

AI may assist research, synthesis, translation, analysis, editing, coding, or visualization, but AI output is not itself evidence.

Material factual claims generated or summarized with AI must be checked against underlying sources before publication.

AI must not fabricate quotations, sources, data, interviews, or observations.

## 8. Conflicts and incentives

Disclose material conflicts of interest, sponsorship, financial exposure, employment relationships, or direct involvement relevant to a story.

Editorial conclusions must not be sold to sponsors.

## 9. Corrections

Significant factual corrections should be explicit and dated.

Corrections should state:

- what was wrong;
- what is correct;
- when the change was made;
- why the correction matters, when material.

## 10. Reproducibility

When a story depends on calculations, code, benchmarks, experiments, or datasets, preserve enough information for an informed reader to inspect or reproduce the result when legally and ethically possible.

## 11. Human dignity

Accuracy does not justify avoidable harm.

Treat people as people, not merely as content. Minimize unnecessary exposure of sensitive personal information and avoid sensationalism where it adds no explanatory value.

## 12. Editorial independence

RESONANCE distinguishes reporting, analysis, opinion, research, and sponsored material.

Readers should never need to guess which one they are reading.

---

**Editorial north star:** publish work that becomes more trustworthy when inspected closely, not less.
